## Product leadership is ultimately a judgment job.

Frameworks can help structure a problem.

Data can help inform a decision.

Customer research can reveal what people need.

But none of them tells you exactly what to do.

That's where product judgment comes in.

Over my career, I've learned to think about products beyond features and roadmaps — as **systems of customer problems, business outcomes, technology, people and trade-offs.**

These are the principles I use to navigate them.

## 01 — PRODUCT JUDGMENT
The hardest product decision is often deciding what not to believe.

I start by asking:

What problem are we actually solving?

Then:

* What evidence tells us this problem matters?
* What assumptions are we making?
* Which assumption could kill the entire bet?
* What would change my mind?
* What is the cost of being wrong?
* What is the smallest experiment that can give us confidence?

I don't believe discovery is only about collecting evidence that supports our idea. **Discovery should also try to break the idea.**

### A lesson I learned the hard way

At Housing.com, I built a broker-facing product around a problem that customers clearly had.

The need was real. The product was useful. Adoption still stalled.

We launched at roughly 25% adoption, improved it to 35% through notifications and outreach, and then plateaued against a 60% stage gate. The bet consumed roughly 24 engineer-months.

The lesson became one of the principles I carry into every new bet:

I validated the need. I didn't validate the trust.

Today, when I evaluate a major product bet, I explicitly map the assumptions that could kill it and design discovery around those assumptions.

## 02 — PRODUCT STRATEGY

### Strategy is choosing where to play — and deliberately choosing where not to.

I don't think strategy is a list of initiatives.

For me, strategy connects:

**Customer problem → business outcome → strategic choices → capabilities → investment → measurement**

One example is how I approached customer care.

The conventional approach was to optimize the bottom of the funnel:

Make agents faster → handle more contacts → reduce cost.

I reframed the system:

**AVOID**

Prevent the customer from needing support.

↓

**AUTOMATE**

Resolve the problem through self-service.

↓

**OPTIMISE**

Make the human interaction better when it is still necessary.

The economics made the strategic choice clear: an agent contact cost roughly $3, self-service around $0.50, while preventing the contact eliminated the service cost altogether.

But the bigger insight wasn't the cost difference.

It was this:

If the customer needs to contact us because something went wrong, optimizing the contact center is treating the symptom.

That changed where I looked for opportunities — including fulfilment, post-order experience, communications and self-service.



## 03 — SYSTEMS THINKING

Customers experience a journey. Organizations manage pieces of it.

One of the most important shifts in my product thinking has been learning to distinguish between:

**What I own**

and

**What causes the outcome I am accountable for.**

Contacts Per Order was a perfect example.

I owned the metric, but many of its causes lived outside Customer Care — in fulfilment, post-order, communications and other parts of the ecommerce journey.

Instead of optimizing my own product area, I created a common view of the customer journey and the friction points across domains.

We then used a common prioritization approach across those problems rather than allowing every organization to optimize its own backlog.

That eventually helped drive a 20% reduction in the first year and 40% across two years.

The principle I took away:

**Don't just optimize your piece of the system when the customer experiences the whole system. **

This is also why I increasingly think in terms of **journeys, systems and mechanisms**, rather than isolated features.

## 04 — PLATFORM THINKING

### Build once. Learn once. Scale many times.

When products operate across markets, teams naturally start asking for variations.

Different regulations, languages, payment behavior, business processes.

The easy answer is:

Build another version.

The platform answer is:

What belongs in the common core, and what should be configurable?

My platform principles are:

**Build once, serve many**

Avoid duplicating fundamentally similar capabilities.

**Core vs. extension points**

Keep the core stable while allowing legitimate market-specific variation.

**Configuration over code**

Where differences are expected, make them configurable rather than creating forks.

**Platform health ≠ feature success**

A platform needs its own measures of leverage, adoption and time-to-onboard.

This thinking has shaped how I've approached customer-care platforms across markets and how I think about product organizations as well.

The deeper principle:

**The real value of a platform isn't what it builds. It's what it makes unnecessary to build again.**

## 05 — CUSTOMER-CENTERED PRODUCT

### Data tells me where to look. Customers tell me what matters.

I value quantitative data enormously.

But I've learned not to confuse measurement with understanding.

A metric can tell you that something changed.

Customer conversations can tell you why.

And sometimes the most important signal is buried in the long tail that dashboards don't surface.

That led me to build a customer-insights capability that combined conversation data, anomaly detection, issue trending and root-cause analysis.

The goal wasn't simply:

**“Tell me what customers are saying.”**

It was:

**“Tell the team that owns the problem what they need to fix.”**

The prototype eventually expanded coverage from roughly 2% of conversations to essentially all of them, while reducing manual review from about three hours a week to thirty minutes. During Canada's migration to the Walmart commerce platform, it surfaced five major issues early enough for teams to act, contributing to approximately $15M in retained GMV.

That experience reinforced one of my favorite product principles:

**Statistics start arguments. Specific orders start fixes.**

## 06 — DECISIONS UNDER AMBIGUITY

You rarely get enough information. Waiting for certainty is itself a decision.

Some of the most consequential product decisions I've made happened without a reliable benchmark.

So instead of asking:

“Do we have enough data to decide?”

I ask:

* What do we know?
* What don't we know?
* Which assumptions matter most?
* What are the options?
* What is the downside of each?
* Can we make the decision reversible?
* What is the cheapest way to learn?

This becomes particularly important when technology is changing faster than historical data can keep up.

My experience moving from deterministic voicebots toward GenAI reinforced this.

We didn't have years of benchmark data telling us exactly what would work.

We had emerging evidence, experiments, technical constraints and customer signals.

When latency reached roughly eight seconds, a 1% experiment showed agent requests increasing from 10% to 20% and customer drops from 2% to 10%. That evidence gave us enough confidence to change the strategy and partner externally rather than continue forcing the internal platform to work.

The principle:

**When certainty isn't available, make the decision smaller, make the learning faster, and make the downside survivable.**

THE THREAD CONNECTING ALL SIX

These aren't six separate philosophies.

They reinforce each other.

**Product Judgment**

Are we solving the right problem?

↓

**Strategy**

Where should we focus our resources?

↓

**Systems Thinking**

What actually drives the outcome?

↓

**Platform Thinking**

What should we build once and leverage repeatedly?

↓

**Customer-Centered Product**

What does reality tell us?

↓

**Judgment Under Ambiguity**

What should we do when reality isn't yet clear?

And ultimately:

## Make the best decision you can. Learn quickly. Adjust deliberately.
