### I like to create enough structure for teams to move fast without creating bureaucracy.

Over the years, I've learned that good execution isn't about pushing people harder. Most teams are capable of working hard. The harder problem is making sure everyone understands what matters, knows what they own, can see problems early and has a way to make decisions without everything coming back to leadership.

My operating style has therefore become fairly simple: **create clarity, establish a rhythm, make ownership visible and keep the organization learning.**

---

### Start with strategy, then work backwards

I don't like starting planning conversations with a list of projects. I prefer to start with the outcome we're trying to create and work backwards. What are we trying to change for the customer or the business? What are the biggest problems standing in the way? Which ones are actually ours to solve? And where should we invest our limited capacity?

From there, I connect longer-term strategy to annual priorities and then to quarterly commitments. **A roadmap should be a consequence of strategy, not a substitute for it.**

---

### Make ownership obvious

One of the simplest things I try to establish early is who owns what. Ambiguity around ownership creates a surprising amount of organizational friction. People can attend the same meetings, discuss the same problem and still walk away assuming someone else will take care of it.

For larger initiatives, I've used clear role definitions across Product, Engineering, UX and Program, along with explicit accountability for outcomes and dependencies. The objective isn't to create a perfect RACI chart. It's to make sure that when something needs to happen, there is no confusion about who is expected to move it forward.

And ownership doesn't mean working alone. It means knowing when to involve others while still remaining accountable for the outcome.

---

### I run on rhythm rather than heroics

This is probably one of the strongest beliefs I've developed about execution. Projects rarely fail because everyone suddenly stopped caring. More often, a dependency wasn't identified, a decision wasn't made or a risk wasn't visible until it was too late.

That's why I like having a regular operating rhythm. Daily stand-ups for the squads or Pods, Weekly reviews help us understand progress and emerging risks, Cross-team forums surface dependencies, Monthly reviews give us a broader view of the business, while quarterly and annual planning gives us the opportunity to step back and ask whether we're still working on the right things.

The exact meetings can change depending on the organization. The principle doesn't: **I want problems to become visible while they are still small enough to solve.**

---

### I want reviews to create decisions, not just updates

As organizations get bigger, it's very easy for product reviews to become a collection of status updates. I've always tried to avoid that. If we're discussing a problem, I want to understand what has changed, what we've learned, what options we have and what decision is needed.

When a PM brings something to a senior leadership forum, I want them to be able to say: Here is the problem. Here is what we've learned. Here are the options. Here is what I recommend.

I'm not looking for a ten-slide description of what happened. **Make the thinking clear. Make the trade-offs visible. Make the recommendation explicit.**

---

### I care more about outcomes than activity

One of the easiest ways for a product organization to fool itself is to become very good at measuring activity — features shipped, projects completed and milestones achieved. Those things matter, but they're not the reason we're doing the work.

I like to establish a clear connection between the outcome we're trying to move, the product experience or customer behaviours that should influence it, and the guardrails we don't want to compromise.

That gives us a much more useful question to ask: **Is this work actually changing the outcome we said mattered?** If it isn't, I'm comfortable challenging the work even if execution against the original plan has been excellent.

---

### I prefer controlled progress to big-bang launches

I've become increasingly uncomfortable with the idea that a product needs to be completely figured out before we put it in front of customers. When the risk is manageable, I'd rather make a smaller bet, learn from it and then decide whether to invest more.

That might mean a prototype, a limited market, a small percentage rollout or testing an assumption before committing significant engineering capacity.

This became particularly relevant as we started working with GenAI. We couldn't rely on years of historical data to tell us exactly what would work. We had to experiment, measure what happened and adjust. In one voicebot experiment, latency became a major problem and early results showed increasing transfers and customer drop-offs. Rather than continuing to invest in an approach that wasn't working, we used the evidence to reconsider the architecture and explore a different path.

**I would rather learn something important early than execute a bad plan perfectly.**

---

### Launch is when the real learning begins

I don't think of launch as the point where Product hands something over to the business. It's when we finally start getting real evidence at scale. I prefer controlled rollouts where we can watch customer and business metrics, identify unexpected behavior and decide whether to expand, change or stop.

This becomes even more important for AI products, where the system can behave differently across a much wider range of situations than traditional software. So the operating model has to include not just launch monitoring, but evaluation, feedback and continuous improvement.

The product isn't finished when we release it. **We're finished an iteration when we've learned enough to know what to do next.**

---

### Keep communication inside the system

I don't want important information to depend on someone remembering to send an update. Regular communication creates context — what's working, what isn't, what changed, what we're learning and where we need help.

This is particularly important with senior stakeholders with whom I propose weekly to monthly cadence based on their involvement level. Difficult conversations become much easier when people have been given context consistently rather than only hearing from you when something is going wrong.

Transparency, for me, isn't something you switch on during a crisis. **It's part of how the organization operates every day.**

---

### What I try to create

At its simplest, my operating model is: Set direction → create ownership → execute → measure → learn → adjust.

The mechanisms around it — planning, reviews, metrics, roadmaps and prioritization — are there to support that loop.

If the mechanisms start getting in the way, I question the mechanisms. The goal isn't an organization that follows process perfectly. It's an organization that can **move quickly, make good decisions and adapt when reality changes.**

When things are working well, people know what matters, they know what they own, problems surface early, teams can operate independently and leaders don't need to be involved in every detail.
